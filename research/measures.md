# South Dakota's 2026 statewide ballot measures

Research current through November 1, 2026. This memo is written for a nonpartisan, plain-language voter guide. It distinguishes a political party's official position from a statement by one officeholder. Campaign-finance totals are snapshots, not final election totals.

## Amendment I: Medicaid expansion and the federal match

### What the question means

South Dakota voters put Medicaid expansion in the state Constitution in 2022. The federal government currently pays 90 percent of the cost for the expansion population and South Dakota pays 10 percent. Amendment I would make that constitutional requirement last only while the federal share stays at 90 percent. A **Yes** vote adds the trigger. A **No** vote leaves the 2022 expansion requirement unchanged. Nothing changes immediately under either result because the federal expansion match remains 90 percent. [2026 ballot-question pamphlet](https://sdsos.gov/elections-voting/upcoming-elections/general-information/2026%20Election%20Information/2026-ballot-questions.aspx) [NCSL summary of the 2025 federal reconciliation law](https://www.ncsl.org/in-dc/tracking-the-2025-budget-reconciliation-process)

There is an important dispute about what would happen if the federal match later fell below 90 percent. Attorney General Marty Jackley says the expansion coverage would end automatically. The measure's legislative sponsors say the constitutional mandate would end, but the Legislature could choose to continue some or all of the program in state law. The amendment's text says the constitutional provision would be effective only while the 90 percent match remains, but it does not spell out a transition process. Voters should understand that the legal effect at the trigger point is contested, not settled. [Enrolled HJR 5001](https://mylrc.sdlegislature.gov/api/Documents/Bill/283708.pdf?Year=2025) [South Dakota Searchlight on the competing interpretations](https://southdakotasearchlight.com/2026/09/18/ballot-measure-could-auto-repeal-medicaid-expansion-attorney-general-says-in-contrast-to-lawmakers/)

### How it reached the ballot

The Legislature placed Amendment I on the ballot through **2025 House Joint Resolution 5001**. Then-Rep. Tony Venhuizen introduced it; the final resolution lists Senate Majority Leader Casey Crabtree and House Majority Leader Will Mortenson as the prime sponsors, and every listed sponsor was a Republican. [HJR 5001 bill page and sponsors](https://sdlegislature.gov/Session/Bill/25683)

| Chamber | Final vote | Party breakdown |
|---|---:|---|
| House | 59-7 | Republicans 59-2; Democrats 0-5. Three Republicans were excused. |
| Senate | 31-3 | Republicans 31-0; Democrats 0-3. One Republican was excused. |

The party counts come from the named roll calls, not just the chamber totals. They show a sharp partisan split, with two House Republicans joining all voting Democrats in opposition. [House roll call](https://sdlegislature.gov/Session/Vote/80476) [Senate roll call](https://sdlegislature.gov/Session/Vote/82140)

### Why it is being proposed now

Voters approved 2022 Amendment D by 192,057 to 149,616, about 56.2 percent to 43.8 percent. It required expansion for adults ages 18 through 64 with incomes up to 138 percent of the federal poverty level. South Dakota began coverage on July 1, 2023. [Secretary of State historical results](https://sdsos.gov/elections-voting/assets/Archive/Prior%20to%202026/BallotQuestions%20%282024%29.pdf) [Department of Social Services Medicaid eligibility page](https://dss.sd.gov/economicassistance/medical_programs.aspx)

The state Department of Social Services reported an average of **29,055 expansion enrollees in state fiscal year 2025**. The 2026 ballot pamphlet rounds the current population to about 30,000, while the opposition campaign has used a more recent figure of about 31,000. The figures cover different reporting periods, so they are not necessarily inconsistent. [DSS 2025 Medicaid report](https://dss.sd.gov/docs/medicaid/reports/2025_Medicaid_Report.pdf) [2026 ballot-question pamphlet](https://sdsos.gov/elections-voting/upcoming-elections/general-information/2026%20Election%20Information/2026-ballot-questions.aspx) [South Dakota Searchlight on the No on I coalition](https://southdakotasearchlight.com/2026/09/29/passing-medicaid-ballot-measure-would-reduce-services-and-spike-insurance-costs-opponents-say/)

HJR 5001 advanced while Congress was debating large Medicaid changes. A February 2025 Congressional Research Service report identified repeal of the Affordable Care Act's enhanced expansion match as one option under discussion. The reconciliation law signed on July 4, 2025 did **not** lower the general 90 percent expansion match. It did add an 80-hour monthly work or community-engagement requirement for many expansion adults beginning January 1, 2027, with exemptions, and it made other Medicaid financing changes. Amendment I therefore responds to a possible future federal cut, not a cut that has already occurred. [CRS Medicaid financing report](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R43847/R43847.12.pdf) [DSS summary of H.R. 1 changes](https://dss.sd.gov/medicaid/HR1.aspx) [NCSL reconciliation tracker](https://www.ncsl.org/in-dc/tracking-the-2025-budget-reconciliation-process) [South Dakota Searchlight on the 2025 legislative debate](https://southdakotasearchlight.com/2025/03/03/lawmakers-will-ask-sd-voters-for-permission-to-end-medicaid-expansion-if-federal-support-declines/)

For scale, the ballot pamphlet's proponents estimate that each 10-percentage-point reduction in the federal share would add about **$36 million a year** to the state's cost. A 20-point fall from 90 percent to 70 percent would therefore add roughly $72 million under that estimate. This is a proponent's example printed in the official pamphlet, not an independent fiscal note or a prediction of a particular federal match. [2026 ballot-question pamphlet](https://sdsos.gov/elections-voting/upcoming-elections/general-information/2026%20Election%20Information/2026-ballot-questions.aspx) [South Dakota Searchlight on the $72 million scenario](https://southdakotasearchlight.com/2025/03/03/lawmakers-will-ask-sd-voters-for-permission-to-end-medicaid-expansion-if-federal-support-declines/)

The Legislature posted **no formal fiscal note** for HJR 5001. That does not mean there could be no fiscal effect. It means there is no official LRC estimate attached to the resolution. The financial effect is contingent on a future federal match reduction and on what the Legislature and courts then decide the amendment requires. ### Practical effect of each vote

**If Yes wins:** Nothing changes as long as the federal government continues paying 90 percent. If that share falls even slightly below 90 percent, the constitutional expansion requirement switches off. Under the attorney general's reading, coverage for the expansion population ends automatically. Under the sponsors' reading, legislators regain the choice to continue, narrow or end the program. The people most exposed to that uncertainty are roughly 30,000 lower-income adults, along with hospitals and clinics that treat them. Hospitals also warn that losing coverage could increase uncompensated care and private insurance costs, especially in rural areas; that is an opponent forecast, not a guaranteed result. [Enrolled HJR 5001](https://mylrc.sdlegislature.gov/api/Documents/Bill/283708.pdf?Year=2025) [Attorney general and sponsor dispute](https://southdakotasearchlight.com/2026/09/18/ballot-measure-could-auto-repeal-medicaid-expansion-attorney-general-says-in-contrast-to-lawmakers/) [Health-provider concerns](https://southdakotasearchlight.com/2026/09/29/passing-medicaid-ballot-measure-would-reduce-services-and-spike-insurance-costs-opponents-say/)

**If No wins:** The Constitution continues to require expansion even if Congress later lowers the match. The state would then need to find more state money, reduce spending elsewhere, raise revenue, or challenge the requirement's operation. A No vote does not block the already-enacted federal work requirements or other federal Medicaid changes. [Current constitutional language and proposed amendment](https://mylrc.sdlegislature.gov/api/Documents/Bill/283708.pdf?Year=2025) [DSS H.R. 1 summary](https://dss.sd.gov/medicaid/HR1.aspx)

### Strongest arguments in good faith

**For Yes:** Voters approved expansion on the understanding that Washington would pay 90 percent. If Congress changes that bargain, elected state lawmakers should be able to reconsider a major spending commitment instead of being constitutionally required to backfill tens of millions of dollars every year. The trigger does not act while the promised match remains in place. [Official pro statement](https://sdsos.gov/elections-voting/upcoming-elections/general-information/2026%20Election%20Information/2026-ballot-questions.aspx)

**For No:** Health coverage should not turn off automatically because Congress makes a budget choice. The amendment creates uncertainty for tens of thousands of people and for health providers, and South Dakota can return to voters with a specific plan if a federal cut actually happens. Writing a one-percentage-point tripwire into the Constitution is a blunt response to an unknown future. [Official con statement](https://sdsos.gov/elections-voting/upcoming-elections/general-information/2026%20Election%20Information/2026-ballot-questions.aspx) [South Dakota Searchlight on the effect dispute](https://southdakotasearchlight.com/2026/09/18/ballot-measure-could-auto-repeal-medicaid-expansion-attorney-general-says-in-contrast-to-lawmakers/)

## Amendment J: citizenship and voting eligibility

### What the question means

The South Dakota Constitution already says every U.S. citizen who meets the state's other qualifications is entitled to vote, and state law already bars noncitizens from registering or voting. Amendment J would add explicit language saying that a person who is not a U.S. citizen is **not qualified to vote in any election in South Dakota**. A **Yes** vote adds that constitutional disqualification. A **No** vote leaves the existing constitutional and statutory bans in place. [Enrolled SJR 503](https://mylrc.sdlegislature.gov/api/Documents/Bill/284225.pdf?Year=2025) [2026 ballot-question pamphlet](https://sdsos.gov/elections-voting/upcoming-elections/general-information/2026%20Election%20Information/2026-ballot-questions.aspx)

The practical distinction is future-proofing. A constitutional ban would prevent the Legislature or a city from authorizing noncitizen voting in state or local elections without another constitutional amendment. The measure does not alter federal citizenship rules and does not itself create a proof-of-citizenship system or a voter-challenge process. [Enrolled SJR 503](https://mylrc.sdlegislature.gov/api/Documents/Bill/284225.pdf?Year=2025) [Attorney general explanation in the pamphlet](https://sdsos.gov/elections-voting/upcoming-elections/general-information/2026%20Election%20Information/2026-ballot-questions.aspx)

### How it reached the ballot

The Legislature placed Amendment J on the ballot through **2025 Senate Joint Resolution 503**. Republican Sen. Taffy Howard and Republican Rep. Logan Manhart were the prime sponsors, and all listed sponsors were Republicans. [SJR 503 bill page and sponsors](https://sdlegislature.gov/Session/Bill/25856)

| Chamber | Final vote | Party breakdown |
|---|---:|---|
| Senate | 33-2 | Republicans 32-0; Democrats 1-2. |
| House | 58-11 | Republicans 57-7; Democrats 1-4. One Republican was excused. |

The roll calls show that Republican legislators were not unanimous: seven House Republicans voted No. Democrats also split, with one Democrat in each chamber voting Yes. [Senate roll call](https://sdlegislature.gov/Session/Vote/80823) [House roll call](https://sdlegislature.gov/Session/Vote/82396)

### Why it is being proposed now

In 2024, the Department of Public Safety compared driver's-license records with voter-registration records and identified 274 people who had marked themselves as noncitizens but appeared on the voter roll. The state checked their status twice through the federal SAVE system. One person had naturalized and remained registered; **273 registrations were removed**. Secretary of State Monae Johnson later said one of those 273 people had cast a ballot. The episode revealed an administrative registration error, not evidence of 273 illegal votes. [Secretary of State response describing the 274 reviews and 273 removals](https://sdsos.gov/about-the-office/assets/Press%20Releases/SOSResponsetoACLU10.11.2024.pdf) [South Dakota Searchlight on the one recorded ballot](https://southdakotasearchlight.com/2026/10/16/closing-a-loophole-or-fearmongering-about-noncitizens-voting-south-dakotas-amendment-j/)

The Legislature has since enacted two separate laws. **2026 Senate Bill 30**, effective July 1, 2026, added lack of U.S. citizenship to the grounds for challenging a registration. **2026 Senate Bill 175** requires documentary proof of citizenship for new state voter registrations and creates a federal-only status for an applicant who cannot provide it. Those laws operate whether Amendment J passes or fails. [SB 30 enrolled text](https://mylrc.sdlegislature.gov/api/Documents/Bill/292265.pdf?Year=2026) [SB 175 session law](https://mylrc.sdlegislature.gov/api/Documents/SessionLaw/306300.pdf?Year=2026)

The pamphlet's con statement says “any registered voter” can challenge another voter. The statute is narrower. A private challenger must be registered **in the same county** as the challenged voter, although the secretary of state or county auditor may also file. A challenge must be filed at least 90 days before an election and supported by a sworn affidavit based on personal knowledge or documented evidence, not an unsupported or anonymous allegation. The auditor investigates, gives the voter 30 days to respond, and offers a hearing. The statute bars cancellation within 90 days before an election and provides an appeal. [Who may challenge and timing, SDCL 12-4-59](https://sdlegislature.gov/Statutes/12-4-59) [Evidence requirements, SDCL 12-4-60](https://sdlegislature.gov/Statutes/12-4-60) [Investigation and cancellation, SDCL 12-4-61](https://sdlegislature.gov/Statutes/12-4-61) [Appeal, SDCL 12-4-62](https://sdlegislature.gov/Statutes/12-4-62)

### Fiscal note

Any costs of proof-of-citizenship checks or registration challenges arise from SB 175 and SB 30, not directly from the amendment. ### Practical effect of each vote

**If Yes wins:** The present ban becomes explicit and constitutionally harder to change. The immediate voting rules remain essentially the same because noncitizens are already barred, new registrants already face documentary requirements, and citizenship challenges are already authorized. Naturalized citizens remain eligible. The people most likely to notice the existing procedures are newly registering citizens who must produce documents and voters whose records are challenged, but those procedures come from the 2026 statutes rather than J itself. [SB 175 session law](https://mylrc.sdlegislature.gov/api/Documents/SessionLaw/306300.pdf?Year=2026) [Challenge statutes](https://sdlegislature.gov/Statutes/12-4-59) [Enrolled SJR 503](https://mylrc.sdlegislature.gov/api/Documents/Bill/284225.pdf?Year=2025)

**If No wins:** Noncitizen voting remains illegal under current law. The 2026 proof and challenge laws also remain. A future Legislature would retain more freedom to change state or local election policy without first amending the Constitution, subject to federal law and the Constitution's existing affirmative language about citizen voters. [2026 ballot-question pamphlet](https://sdsos.gov/elections-voting/upcoming-elections/general-information/2026%20Election%20Information/2026-ballot-questions.aspx) [SB 30 enrolled text](https://mylrc.sdlegislature.gov/api/Documents/Bill/292265.pdf?Year=2026) [SB 175 session law](https://mylrc.sdlegislature.gov/api/Documents/SessionLaw/306300.pdf?Year=2026)

### Strongest arguments in good faith

**For Yes:** Voting is a core act of citizenship. The 2024 registration errors show that a clear rule matters, and putting the prohibition in the Constitution prevents a future state or local government from weakening it through ordinary legislation. Supporters view the amendment as a simple, durable statement rather than a response that depends on current officeholders. [Official pro statement](https://sdsos.gov/elections-voting/upcoming-elections/general-information/2026%20Election%20Information/2026-ballot-questions.aspx) [Secretary of State response on the 2024 removals](https://sdsos.gov/about-the-office/assets/Press%20Releases/SOSResponsetoACLU10.11.2024.pdf)

**For No:** The rule is already clear in state law, and the Legislature has already added proof and challenge systems. A redundant constitutional amendment can amplify suspicion of lawful voters without solving an uncovered legal gap. Opponents also worry about burdens on naturalized citizens and erroneous challenges, even though the challenge statute contains timing, evidence, hearing and appeal protections. [Official con statement](https://sdsos.gov/elections-voting/upcoming-elections/general-information/2026%20Election%20Information/2026-ballot-questions.aspx) [Challenge safeguards](https://sdlegislature.gov/Statutes/12-4-60) [South Dakota Searchlight explainer](https://southdakotasearchlight.com/2026/10/16/closing-a-loophole-or-fearmongering-about-noncitizens-voting-south-dakotas-amendment-j/)

## Amendment K: a constitutional trust for unclaimed property

### What the question means

Banks, insurers and other businesses turn over property to the state when they lose contact with its owner. Examples include dormant accounts, uncashed checks, securities and insurance proceeds. Owners or heirs keep the right to claim the property. Amendment K would put the state's net unclaimed-property receipts into a constitutional trust, allow the State Investment Council to invest the money, and send a statutory share of investment earnings to the general fund beginning July 1, 2027. [South Dakota unclaimed-property statutes](https://sdlegislature.gov/Statutes/43-41B) [Enrolled SJR 505](https://mylrc.sdlegislature.gov/api/Documents/Bill/284191.pdf?Year=2025)

The trust would be protected, but not untouchable. Principal and earnings could be used to pay claims and return property. They could be used for another purpose only with approval from three-fourths of **all members** of each legislative chamber. The amendment leaves the annual distribution formula to state law. [Enrolled SJR 505](https://mylrc.sdlegislature.gov/api/Documents/Bill/284191.pdf?Year=2025)

### How it reached the ballot

The Legislature placed Amendment K on the ballot through **2025 Senate Joint Resolution 505**. Republican Sen. Taffy Howard and Republican Rep. Chris Kassin were the prime sponsors. The sponsor list was bipartisan and included Democratic legislators Red Dawn Foster and Eric Emery. [SJR 505 bill page and sponsors](https://sdlegislature.gov/Session/Bill/25479)

| Chamber | Final vote | Party breakdown |
|---|---:|---|
| Senate | 35-0 | Republicans 32-0; Democrats 3-0. |
| House | 69-0 | Republicans 64-0; Democrats 5-0. One Republican was excused. |

K was the only one of the four ballot referrals to pass both chambers unanimously among members voting. [Senate roll call](https://sdlegislature.gov/Session/Vote/81598) [House roll call](https://sdlegislature.gov/Session/Vote/82375)

### Why it is being proposed now

Unclaimed property has become a large and volatile source of state revenue, partly because many nationally operating financial businesses are legally domiciled in South Dakota. A 2025 state treasurer handout listed **$1.191 billion in total claimable property**, 957,394 claimable properties, and **$98.27 million attributable to South Dakota claimants**. It said the state had received $598.69 million during the preceding three years and that claims remain payable forever. The large gap between total property and the portion attributed to South Dakotans reflects the program's national reach. [State treasurer handout](https://mylrc.sdlegislature.gov/api/Documents/Attachment/282470.pdf?Year=2025)

The Bureau of Finance and Management reported general-fund unclaimed-property receipts of $85.5 million in fiscal year 2023, $133.6 million in 2024 and an estimated $247.3 million in 2025, much of it treated as one-time money. The audited fiscal year 2025 report separated $60.253 million of ongoing receipts from $223.720 million in one-time proceeds from property sales. These are flows in particular years, not the same thing as the total balance owed to all potential claimants. [BFM fiscal year 2026 revenue estimates](https://bfm.sd.gov/budget/FY2026/RevenueEstimates20250212.pdf) [South Dakota fiscal year 2025 annual comprehensive financial report](https://bfm.sd.gov/acfr/SD_ACFR_2025.PDF)

South Dakota already created a **statutory** trust through 2025 Senate Bill 155. Current law lets the operating fund retain up to $500,000, directs a scheduled amount to the general fund, and deposits the excess in the trust. The general-fund cap steps down over time, reaching $25 million in fiscal year 2035 and later. A separate section generally distributes 4 percent of the trust's market value without invading principal. Amendment K would place the framework in the Constitution and allow the State Investment Council to manage it like other long-term state trusts. [SB 155 enrolled text](https://mylrc.sdlegislature.gov/api/Documents/Bill/282223.pdf?Year=2025) [Current deposit rule, SDCL 43-41B-24](https://sdlegislature.gov/Statutes/43-41B-24) [Current distribution rule, SDCL 43-41B-24.5](https://sdlegislature.gov/Statutes/43-41B-24.5)

That existing law matters for the ballot choice. A No vote would not immediately return South Dakota to a system of spending every available dollar. It would leave the statutory trust in place, but a future Legislature could change it by ordinary law. [Current deposit rule](https://sdlegislature.gov/Statutes/43-41B-24) [Enrolled SJR 505](https://mylrc.sdlegislature.gov/api/Documents/Bill/284191.pdf?Year=2025)

### Fiscal note

The Legislature posted **no formal fiscal note** for SJR 505. The official pro statement says the temporary fund earns roughly 3 to 4 percent while comparable constitutional trusts have earned roughly 7.9 to 8.3 percent over the cited periods. Those are the treasurer's performance comparisons, not guaranteed future returns. Investment values and annual earnings can rise or fall. [LRC fiscal-note endpoint for SJR 505](https://sdlegislature.gov/api/Bills/FiscalNotes/25479) [Official pro statement](https://sdsos.gov/elections-voting/upcoming-elections/general-information/2026%20Election%20Information/2026-ballot-questions.aspx)

### Positions, coalitions and money

- The **South Dakota Democratic Party lists no position** on K. That is different from opposing it. Democratic legislators voted unanimously for the referral. ### Practical effect of each vote

**If Yes wins:** Net receipts build a constitutionally protected long-term fund. The State Investment Council can invest for long-run returns, claims continue to be paid, and a statutory portion of earnings supports the general fund. Future legislators have less freedom to spend principal during a budget crunch, though three-fourths of all members in each chamber could authorize another use. Claimants are still entitled to their property. Taxpayers and programs supported by the general fund bear the upside and risk of variable investment earnings. [Enrolled SJR 505](https://mylrc.sdlegislature.gov/api/Documents/Bill/284191.pdf?Year=2025)

**If No wins:** The statutory trust and its present deposit and distribution rules remain. Legislators could later amend or repeal those rules with an ordinary bill, and the money would not receive constitutional protection or necessarily the same investment treatment. Owners' right to file claims would remain unchanged. [Current trust statutes](https://sdlegislature.gov/Statutes/43-41B-24) [Current distribution statute](https://sdlegislature.gov/Statutes/43-41B-24.5) [South Dakota unclaimed-property chapter](https://sdlegislature.gov/Statutes/43-41B)

### Strongest arguments in good faith

**For Yes:** The state holds the money subject to claims that never expire, so it should preserve a durable principal instead of treating unpredictable receipts as ordinary revenue. Constitutional protection makes it harder for a future Legislature to raid the fund, while professional long-term investing can produce earnings for public services. [Official pro statement](https://sdsos.gov/elections-voting/upcoming-elections/general-information/2026%20Election%20Information/2026-ballot-questions.aspx) [State treasurer handout](https://mylrc.sdlegislature.gov/api/Documents/Attachment/282470.pdf?Year=2025)

**For No:** South Dakota already has a statutory trust, and budget and investment rules may need adjustment as claim patterns, court rulings and markets change. Putting a detailed financial structure in the Constitution reduces flexibility, while expected higher returns are not guaranteed. The three-fourths exception and Legislature-set payout formula also mean the constitutional lock is not absolute. This is a synthesized best-case argument because no formal con statement or organized opposition was filed. [Current trust statute](https://sdlegislature.gov/Statutes/43-41B-24) [Enrolled SJR 505](https://mylrc.sdlegislature.gov/api/Documents/Bill/284191.pdf?Year=2025) [2026 ballot-question pamphlet](https://sdsos.gov/elections-voting/upcoming-elections/general-information/2026%20Election%20Information/2026-ballot-questions.aspx)

## Amendment L: a 60 percent threshold for future constitutional amendments

### What the question means

South Dakota constitutional amendments currently pass with more Yes votes than No votes. Amendment L would require **at least 60 percent Yes** for every future constitutional amendment, whether proposed by citizens or referred by the Legislature. It would not change the threshold for initiated statutes or referred laws. A proposal with 59 percent Yes would fail. [Enrolled HJR 5003](https://mylrc.sdlegislature.gov/api/Documents/Bill/284377.pdf?Year=2025) [2026 ballot-question pamphlet](https://sdsos.gov/elections-voting/upcoming-elections/general-information/2026%20Election%20Information/2026-ballot-questions.aspx)

Amendment L itself needs only a simple majority because the 60 percent rule is not yet in force. It would not retroactively change the outcome of I, J or K on the same ballot. If L passes, the higher threshold applies to constitutional amendments at future elections. [Associated Press explanation](https://apnews.com/article/2be9e9c2900d17d93ac588b136039d56) [Enrolled HJR 5003](https://mylrc.sdlegislature.gov/api/Documents/Bill/284377.pdf?Year=2025)

### How it reached the ballot

The Legislature placed Amendment L on the ballot through **2025 House Joint Resolution 5003**. Republican Rep. John Hughes and Republican Sen. Sue Peterson were the prime sponsors, and all listed sponsors were Republicans. The introduced resolution required two-thirds, but lawmakers amended it to 60 percent before passage. [HJR 5003 bill page and sponsors](https://sdlegislature.gov/Session/Bill/25663) [Official amendment changing two-thirds to 60 percent](https://mylrc.sdlegislature.gov/api/Documents/AmendmentInstructions/275690.html?Year=2025)

| Chamber | Final vote | Party breakdown |
|---|---:|---|
| House | 61-5 | Republicans 61-0; Democrats 0-5. Three Republicans were excused. |
| Senate | 29-4 | Republicans 29-2; Democrats 0-2. One Republican and one Democrat were excused. |

Two Republican senators joined the voting Democrats against the referral. Republicans were therefore strongly supportive but not unanimous. [House roll call](https://sdlegislature.gov/Session/Vote/80516) [Senate roll call](https://sdlegislature.gov/Session/Vote/82452)

### Earlier threshold proposals and recent ballot measures

South Dakota voters have twice rejected related supermajority proposals. **2018 Amendment X** would have required 55 percent for constitutional amendments and failed with 140,730 Yes and 163,362 No, about 46.3 percent Yes. **2022 Amendment C** would have required 60 percent for ballot measures that raise taxes or obligate $10 million or more over five years; it failed in the June primary with 59,125 Yes and 122,417 No, about 32.6 percent Yes. L is broader than both because it applies to every future constitutional amendment and does not apply to ordinary initiated laws. [Secretary of State ballot-question history](https://sdsos.gov/elections-voting/assets/Archive/Prior%20to%202026/BallotQuestions%20%282024%29.pdf) [Enrolled HJR 5003](https://mylrc.sdlegislature.gov/api/Documents/Bill/284377.pdf?Year=2025)

Recent results show which outcomes a 60 percent rule would and would not have changed:

| Measure | Yes share and result under the rules then | Result if L's rule had applied |
|---|---|---|
| 2022 Amendment D, Medicaid expansion | About 56.2%, passed | Would have failed |
| 2024 Amendment G, abortion rights | About 41.4%, failed | Still would have failed |
| 2020 Amendment A, recreational marijuana | About 54.2%, passed, then struck down by courts | Would have failed at the ballot |
| 2020 Initiated Measure 26, medical marijuana | About 69.9%, passed | Not covered because it was a statute |
| 2022 Initiated Measure 27 and 2024 Initiated Measure 29, recreational marijuana statutes | Both failed | Not covered because they were statutes |

The historical totals and measure types come from the Secretary of State. The counterfactual results follow directly from L's 60 percent threshold. Amendment A's later court invalidation was based on the Constitution's single-subject and revision rules, not its share of the vote. [Secretary of State ballot-question history](https://sdsos.gov/elections-voting/assets/Archive/Prior%20to%202026/BallotQuestions%20%282024%29.pdf) [South Dakota Supreme Court decision on Amendment A](https://ujs.sd.gov/media/55uad2hu/2954698e671a.pdf) [Enrolled HJR 5003](https://mylrc.sdlegislature.gov/api/Documents/Bill/284377.pdf?Year=2025)

### Practical effect of each vote

**If Yes wins:** Every future constitutional amendment needs 60 percent. That makes both citizen initiatives and legislative referrals harder to pass. A 59-41 majority would lose, so more than 40 percent of voters could block a change. The rule does not make initiated statutes harder to pass, but some subjects require constitutional treatment and a statute cannot override the Constitution. Groups seeking change may respond by using statutes when legally possible or by building broader coalitions. [Enrolled HJR 5003](https://mylrc.sdlegislature.gov/api/Documents/Bill/284377.pdf?Year=2025) [South Dakota Searchlight explainer](https://southdakotasearchlight.com/2026/10/14/protecting-the-state-constitution-or-protecting-voters-right-to-change-it-amendment-l-explained/)

**If No wins:** Future amendments continue to pass by simple majority. Citizens and legislators retain the same rules, including existing signature, single-subject and ballot-review requirements. A No vote does not make constitutional amendments easy to place on the ballot; it preserves only the current approval threshold once a measure qualifies. [Secretary of State initiative guidance](https://sdsos.gov/elections-voting/upcoming-elections/general-information/ballot-question-process.aspx) [Current and proposed constitutional text](https://mylrc.sdlegislature.gov/api/Documents/Bill/284377.pdf?Year=2025)

### Strongest arguments in good faith

**For Yes:** A constitution sets the durable rules of government and should not change with a narrow, temporary majority. A 60 percent threshold forces broader agreement, discourages expensive national groups from using South Dakota's Constitution for ordinary policy, and still leaves the initiative process available for statutes. The rule also binds amendments proposed by the Legislature, not only citizen measures. [Official pro statement](https://sdsos.gov/elections-voting/upcoming-elections/general-information/2026%20Election%20Information/2026-ballot-questions.aspx) [South Dakota Searchlight explainer](https://southdakotasearchlight.com/2026/10/14/protecting-the-state-constitution-or-protecting-voters-right-to-change-it-amendment-l-explained/)

**For No:** The rule lets 41 percent defeat what 59 percent support and weakens a direct-democracy power South Dakotans have used when the Legislature would not act. Existing rules already require signatures and a statewide majority, and voters rejected related supermajority proposals in 2018 and 2022. Outside money can be used to defend the status quo as well as to change it, so the threshold does not remove national influence. [Official con statement](https://sdsos.gov/elections-voting/upcoming-elections/general-information/2026%20Election%20Information/2026-ballot-questions.aspx) [Secretary of State historical results](https://sdsos.gov/elections-voting/assets/Archive/Prior%20to%202026/BallotQuestions%20%282024%29.pdf) [South Dakota Searchlight campaign-finance report](https://southdakotasearchlight.com/2026/10/23/national-groups-power-spending-against-higher-bar-for-constitutional-amendments/)

## Did any citizen measures or referred laws qualify?

**No. The statewide November 3, 2026 ballot contains only Amendments I, J, K and L, all referred by the Legislature.** The Secretary of State's final ballot-question page and official pamphlet list only those four qualified questions. The same web page separately labels other proposals as merely “approved for circulation,” which is not ballot qualification. [Secretary of State 2026 ballot-question page](https://sdsos.gov/elections-voting/upcoming-elections/general-information/2026%20Election%20Information/2026-ballot-questions.aspx)

The filing deadline confirms it. On May 6, 2026, the Secretary of State announced that **no citizen ballot-question petitions were filed by the May 5 deadline** and that “there will be no additional ballot questions” for the 2026 general election. That rules out citizen-initiated constitutional amendments, initiated laws and referenda, including the discussed school-prayer, property-tax, single-subject and raw-milk proposals. Some proposals received a title or permission to circulate, but none submitted enough valid signatures by the deadline. [Secretary of State May 6 press release](https://www.sdsos.gov/about-the-office/assets/Press%20Releases/No%20Ballot%20Questions%20.pdf) [Secretary of State press-release index](https://www.sdsos.gov/about-the-office/Press-Release.aspx)

Three citizen amendments had reached the circulation stage in late 2025, concerning protections for the initiative process, a seven-year protection period and a property-tax or retail-transaction-tax proposal. Each needed 35,017 valid signatures. None was filed by the deadline. A referendum petition concerning 2026 SB 245 was also approved for circulation but did not qualify. The definitive point is the Secretary of State's post-deadline statement and final ballot list, not whether a proposal had earlier paperwork. [Secretary of State circulation announcement](https://sdsos.gov/about-the-office/assets/Press%20Releases/PetitionPressRelease.pdf) [Final 2026 ballot-question page](https://sdsos.gov/elections-voting/upcoming-elections/general-information/2026%20Election%20Information/2026-ballot-questions.aspx)

## Cross-measure cautions for a voter guide

- A **Yes** vote always means adopting the printed amendment; a **No** vote means leaving the current Constitution unchanged. All four amendments need only a simple majority at this election. [2026 ballot-question pamphlet](https://sdsos.gov/elections-voting/upcoming-elections/general-information/2026%20Election%20Information/2026-ballot-questions.aspx)
- No formal legislative fiscal note was posted for any of the four referrals. The lack of a note is not a finding of zero cost. I and K can have large contingent financial effects, while L can alter which future measures pass. [Fiscal notes for I](https://sdlegislature.gov/api/Bills/FiscalNotes/25683) [J](https://sdlegislature.gov/api/Bills/FiscalNotes/25856) [K](https://sdlegislature.gov/api/Bills/FiscalNotes/25479) [L](https://sdlegislature.gov/api/Bills/FiscalNotes/25663)
- The SDGOP officially supports all four. The SDDP officially opposes I, J and L and takes no position on K. Legislative voting was less uniform than those party recommendations: Republicans split on I, J and L, Democrats split on J, and K was unanimous. [SDGOP recommendations](https://www.sdgop.com/2026-ballot-questions/) [SDDP recommendations](https://www.sddp.org/2026-midterm-elections) [Legislative vote links collected on each bill page](https://sdlegislature.gov/Session/Bills/70)
